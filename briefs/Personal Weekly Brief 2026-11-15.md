# Personal Weekly Brief, 2026-11-15

## Books and Reading

### 1. Dominion: An Anthology of Speculative Fiction from Africa and the African Diaspora, edited by Zelda Knight and Ekpeki Oghenechovwe Donald (lead pick)
- **Type:** Short fiction anthology, 13 stories, Africanfuturist and diasporic speculative fiction
- **Date:** Aurelia Leo, 2020
- **Source:** Tangent Online review; BSFA review by Fiona Moore; Wikipedia entry. It carries a foreword by Tananarive Due, and trade listings report a British Fantasy Award win for Best Anthology and a Locus Poll nomination.
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** This is the best single sampler of the lane you care most about, and it is a fast way to find your next author. Stories include Dilman Dila's "Red Bati" (a robot in the form of a dog), Rafeeat Aliyu, Suyi Davies Okungbowa, Marian Denise Moore and Eugen Bacon. Note that the anthology includes a few darker reprints, so skip those if they lean toward horror. I could not find a complete verified table of contents, so the story list above is partial.
- **Connects to:** Lagos 2060 and Omenana (earlier briefs), and the Flame Tree anthology from your earlier list. Wole Talabi's work is a natural neighbor.

### 2. Babel-17, Samuel R. Delany
- **Type:** Novel, science fiction (often paired with the novella Empire Star)
- **Date:** Ace Books, 1966. It won the Nebula Award for Best Novel; sources label the award year 1966 or 1967 (it tied with Flowers for Algernon), so I am noting the disagreement.
- **Source:** SFWA Nebula Awards database; publisher editions
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** A poet and starship captain, Rydra Wong, decodes an enemy language that works as a weapon. It is a language-and-power puzzle from one of the founders of Black American science fiction, and Delany has a huge backlist (Nova, Dhalgren, the Return to Nevèrÿon sagas) if he clicks. It lines up with Serpell and Butler for readers who like ideas with a human center. I did not find a source calling him "the first" major Black SF author, so I am not making that claim.
- **Connects to:** Wild Seed (10-25 brief), as two foundations of the field.

### 3. Unmasking AI: My Mission to Protect What Is Human in a World of Machines, Joy Buolamwini
- **Type:** Memoir and nonfiction, technology and AI
- **Date:** Random House, October 2023 (from memory; confirm your edition)
- **Source:** Random House / Penguin Random House
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** Buolamwini is a Black computer scientist and founder of the Algorithmic Justice League. The book follows how she found that facial analysis systems failed on darker faces. It is a strong companion to Race After Technology (11-08 brief), but more personal and more story-driven.
- **Connects to:** Coded Bias below, the documentary she appears in.

## Watch

### 4. The Brother from Another Planet, John Sayles
- **Type:** Feature film, science fiction comedy-drama
- **Date:** 1984
- **Source:** Criterion Channel trailer page; JustWatch and Moviefone listings
- **Availability:** Available elsewhere
- **Why for you:** Joe Morton plays an alien who escapes slavery and lands in New York Harbor, then lands in Harlem. It is quiet, funny, and a classic of Black cinematic science fiction (Ernest Dickerson shot it). Aggregators disagree on where it streams (Criterion Channel, Kanopy, Tubi, Prime Video and others are all listed), and the Criterion listing may be stale. Check before paying.
- **Connects to:** Space Is the Place (11-01 brief), a very different take on a Black arrival story.

### 5. Coded Bias, Shalini Kantayya
- **Type:** Documentary, about 90 minutes
- **Date:** 2020
- **Source:** Aggregator listings (JustWatch, Yidio) suggest free library streaming on Kanopy and Hoopla, and Netflix at some point. I did not verify any of this in a service listing.
- **Availability:** Available elsewhere
- **Why for you:** Follows Joy Buolamwini's discovery of bias in facial recognition, with other women researchers. It is a direct, watchable entry into your technology and AI lane. Check Hoopla in your own library app, since it may be free there.
- **Connects to:** Unmasking AI above.

## Learn

### 6. NLÉ, Kunlé Adeyemi, and the Makoko Floating School
- **Type:** Architecture practice and project archive, with a notable story of a floating school in a Lagos lagoon community
- **Date:** School completed March 2013; collapsed June 7, 2016; shortlisted for the Aga Khan Award for Architecture (2014 to 2016 cycle); NLÉ won a 2016 Venice Biennale Silver Lion for a Promising Young Participant, which sources tie to a recreated version.
- **Source:** NLÉ project description (August 2013); Aga Khan Development Network; Africa News; Architect Magazine; The Atavist "Things Fall Apart"
- **Availability:** Worth putting on my radar
- **Why for you:** Design where the ideas come from water-based Lagos building and community: A-frame timber, local labor, and a school built for people the city ignored. It extends your Kere, Kamara and Worofila thread to coastal West Africa. Adeyemi is Nigerian. I did not check the current NLÉ site.
- **Connects to:** Mariam Kamara (11-08 brief) and Lagoon (10-18 brief), for Lagos itself.

## Rabbit Hole of the Week: Black arrival stories, from Delany to the Harlem alien

Read Babel-17, then watch The Brother from Another Planet and note how both turn "arriving somewhere you cannot read" into a political question. Add Space Is the Place (11-01) and Dominion for a decades-long thread: Delany in 1966, Sayles in 1984, Sun Ra in 1974, and the new African voices in 2020. For the real-world mirror, read about Makoko, where a community built what the city would not.
