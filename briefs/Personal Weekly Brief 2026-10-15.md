# Personal Weekly Brief, 2026-10-15

## Books and Reading

### 1. Sweep of Stars, Maurice Broaddus (lead pick)
- **Type:** Novel, Afrofuturist space opera, book one of a trilogy
- **Date:** Tor Books, March 29, 2022 (paperback early 2023)
- **Source:** Tor Books / Macmillan; Publishers Weekly starred review called it a "powerful, sweeping Afrofuturist space opera"
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** It follows a Black American family from the Muungano, a coalition of city-states built after leaving Old Earth, out to Mars and Titan around the year 2121. It is big, multigenerational, and politically alive, which suits your Afrofuturism lane. It is also the first of a trilogy, so there is a backlist to binge. Library Journal and other trade sources call it the start of a trilogy, and it was reported as a 2023 Locus Award finalist.
- **Connects to:** Afronauts below. Both ask who gets to imagine themselves into space.

### 2. Where the False Gods Dwell, Denny S. Bryce
- **Type:** Historical fiction
- **Date:** Kensington, February 24, 2026
- **Source:** Kensington Books
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** Katherine Dunham's 1930s research voyage to Jamaica, told through three fictional women: a Chicago con artist, an anthropologist, and a Jamaican labor activist. The expedition reaches the Maroon village of Accompong. It is dance, diaspora, and history, with a survival plot. Note the author is Denny S. Bryce, and Dunham appears as a character.
- **Connects to:** Rabbit Hole below, a different trail of Black people reaching beyond their assigned maps.

### 3. Happy Land, Dolen Perkins-Valdez
- **Type:** Historical fiction
- **Date:** Hardcover April 8, 2025; paperback April 2026 (Hachette India lists April 2, 2026, so check your edition)
- **Source:** Amistad / HarperCollins (US); Phoenix (UK paperback)
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** A woman traces her family to the Kingdom of the Happy Land, a real community of formerly enslaved people in the North Carolina mountains. Perkins-Valdez excels at the quiet cost of freedom. It is a good counterweight to the space opera: the same question of building a world on your own terms, set in the soil.

## Watch

### 4. Afronauts, Frances Bodomo
- **Type:** Short film, about 12 to 14 minutes (sources vary)
- **Date:** 2014
- **Source:** Sloan Science & Film; also released free by Vernac Media and Boiler Room 4:3 for the Apollo 11 anniversary
- **Availability:** Freely available online
- **Why for you:** A fictionalized look at Zambia's 1964 space academy, in moody black and white. It is short, striking, and a perfect Africanfuturist appetizer. Bodomo is Ghanaian-American.
- **Connects to:** Rabbit Hole below.

### 5. The Last Angel of History, John Akomfrah (Black Audio Film Collective)
- **Type:** Documentary essay film, about 45 minutes
- **Date:** 1995 (some sources say 1996)
- **Source:** Criterion Channel listed it in an Afrofuturism collection per a 2021 report; Icarus Films distributes it
- **Availability:** Available elsewhere
- **Why for you:** The foundational film of Afrofuturism, tracing Black music and technology through a "Data Thief" who searches for fragments of the future. Watch it after Afronauts for the theory behind the feeling. I could not confirm current streaming, so check Criterion or a library or museum screening.

## Rabbit Hole of the Week: The Zambian Space Academy and the Black space imagination

Start with Afronauts, then notice that its source story is real: in 1964, as Zambia gained independence, Edward Makuka Nkoloso publicly promoted a homegrown space program to beat the US and USSR to the moon. Bodomo's film is fictionalized from that. Follow the thread to The Last Angel of History, then Sweep of Stars, and you have a one-weekend path from archive, to theory, to novel. Dunham's Jamaica voyage in Where the False Gods Dwell is the historical-fiction side trail.
