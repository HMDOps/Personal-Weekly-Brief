# Personal Weekly Brief, 2026-11-08

## Books and Reading

### 1. The Old Drift, Namwali Serpell (lead pick)
- **Type:** Novel, multigenerational Zambian epic with speculative and Afrofuturist threads (debut)
- **Date:** Hogarth, 2019. Sources differ on the exact day (March 21 and March 26, 2019 both appear, with a paperback listed April 2, 2019 and another in 2020), so confirm your edition.
- **Source:** Penguin Random House; Wikipedia and SF Encyclopedia entries on Serpell. Winner of the 2020 Anisfield-Wolf Book Award in Fiction and the 2020 Arthur C. Clarke Award.
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** Three interwoven families in Zambia, from Northern Rhodesia around 1900 into the near future, told with a big, wry, genre-bending voice. Serpell was born in Lusaka, and the book won the UK's top science fiction prize while also being literary fiction to the bone. On Morrison is already on your list, so this is the same mind at novel length. It also picks up the Zambia thread from Afronauts in the 10-15 brief.
- **Connects to:** Afronauts (earlier brief) and The Fifth Season below, both about whole worlds built across generations.

### 2. The Fifth Season, N. K. Jemisin
- **Type:** Novel, science fantasy, book one of the Broken Earth trilogy
- **Date:** Orbit, August 4, 2015
- **Source:** Hachette / Orbit; Bookshop.org. Won the 2016 Hugo Award for Best Novel, and the trilogy made Jemisin the first author to win the Best Novel Hugo three years running.
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** A continent that keeps breaking apart, a society built on the enslavement of people with a dangerous power, and a mother searching for her stolen child. It is the biggest bingeable Black-authored series you have not yet been handed, with two more books (The Obelisk Gate, August 16, 2016, and The Stone Sky). Jemisin is new to these briefs. It sits between Butler's power dynamics and Okorafor's world-building.
- **Connects to:** Wild Seed and An Unkindness of Ghosts (earlier briefs), on bondage and power in speculative worlds.

### 3. Indigo, Beverly Jenkins
- **Type:** Historical romance, Underground Railroad era
- **Date:** Avon, 1996 (one review site gives 2000, likely a later edition)
- **Source:** Library catalog record (Avon, 1996); reviews noting Jenkins's research
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** Hester Wyatt, a Michigan conductor on the Underground Railroad, and the mysterious Galen Vachon, "Black Daniel." Jenkins is the grande dame of Black historical romance and has a deep backlist, so this is a bingeable doorway. Some readers note the romance meets little resistance and the Railroad plot carries the book, which suits a historical-fiction reader. Jenkins also has a newer Blessings series (Calling All Blessings, late 2025) if you want contemporary.
- **Connects to:** An Extraordinary Union (11-01 brief), the modern descendant of the genre Jenkins built.

### 4. Race After Technology: Abolitionist Tools for the New Jim Code, Ruha Benjamin
- **Type:** Nonfiction, technology and AI
- **Date:** Polity, 2019 (listings give May and June 2019 for the hardback and paperback)
- **Source:** Polity; Times Higher Education review. Benjamin is an Associate Professor of African American Studies at Princeton.
- **Availability:** Check catalog
- **Why for you:** Your technology and AI lane, from an African American scholar. Benjamin argues that technology can quietly reproduce racial hierarchy, and offers tools for pushing back. It reads well alongside the speculative fiction on your list, because it asks what a designed future will actually carry forward.
- **Connects to:** Masakhane (earlier pick) and Neptune Frost, for Black technological imagination at work.

## Watch

### 5. Mariam Kamara on changing narratives through architecture, Design Indaba 2019
- **Type:** Conference talk (length not confirmed)
- **Date:** 2019, Cape Town
- **Source:** Design Indaba Conference 2019; ArchDaily interview "Design can be a Powerful Tool for Good"
- **Availability:** Available elsewhere
- **Why for you:** Kamara is a Niger-born architect and founder of atelier masōmī (the name is Hausa for "inception"), which won the Aga Khan Award for Architecture in 2022. She uses local materials and Hausa building ideas, which fits your Afrocentric design lane. I could not confirm a free stream, so search the Design Indaba site and YouTube.
- **Connects to:** Francis Kere's TED talk and Worofila interview (earlier picks).

## Learn

### 6. Brittle Paper
- **Type:** Online literary magazine and news hub for African literature, free to read
- **Date:** Founded 2010 by Ainehi Edoro, a Nigerian scholar at the University of Wisconsin-Madison; ongoing
- **Source:** brittlepaper.com; University of Wisconsin profile
- **Availability:** Freely available online
- **Why for you:** The best single place to learn what is being published in African literature right now, including its annual "100 Notable African Books" list. It is a steady source for picks like these, and includes pieces on Africanfuturism. I did not browse the current front page, so check what is newest.
- **Connects to:** Omenana (10-25 brief), a more speculative-fiction-specific sibling.

## Rabbit Hole of the Week: Mariam Kamara and the architecture of Niger

Start with the Design Indaba talk, where Kamara closed with the Artisan's Valley project in Niamey, a collaboration between atelier masōmī and Design Indaba. Then read the ArchDaily interviews, and follow her lectures at Harvard GSD (March 7, 2022), MIT (April 8, 2021) and Columbia (October 4, 2021). It is a way into how contemporary West African architecture is argued about, and a natural next stop after Kere's work with clay and Worofila. For a fiction counterpoint, Serpell's Old Drift builds a whole country on the page.
